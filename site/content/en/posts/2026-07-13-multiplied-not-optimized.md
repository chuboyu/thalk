LLM-based AI coding agents has been booming for a while, and you know it when all of your non-coder friends are generating codes and deploying services.
For software engineers this is natural: "someone" can do their job for them, releasing more time for something much more valuable. Interestingly, there is a huge gap between the addicted and the reluctant coders.

I myself, like other heavy users, experienced nearly 100x in delivering outcomes, while some others report that this hardly change their life.

The mismatch is solid, and it reminds me of the time when I first learned how to ride a bicycle on my own. The wind soaring past my hair, and I could "sprint" far faster than I could ever run. The sense of freedom rushing through every veins, as if I can go to anywhere. 

"This is great" I told my dad. 
"It truly is" he replied, "But don't limit yourself from other greatness. You'll also learn to ride a motorcycle, drive a car, or even fly a plane or launch rockets."
He paused, and say the words that are still a core part of me.

**"You can't reach the stars by mastering the art of bike riding."**

So what does utilizing coding agents have to do with this.

You would think that the gap emerges from how effective they are utilizing this new "tool" or it lacking corresponding domain knowledges. However, my chat with many, coders and non-coders, all reflect similar speed-up magnitude (roughly 10x) and still requires human supervision if you wish to maintain certain quality. In my own experience, an implementation that used to take 3 days to complete is now roughly 3 hours. This is of course already a huge improvement, but obviously, far from the 100x boost some of us experienced.

So what's different? Are my claims contradicting?

No, and in a more radical thinking, I believe that the boost can go up to 1000x under the correct (but rare) condiction.
The gap comes from how you evaluate the speed improvement. Implementing a single feature is one thing, and delivering comprehensive outcome is another. 
If I define this to be a "code generator", then the best I am getting is a 10x boost. 

But a delivery is not just "printing the codes".

If you view LLM as a "re-structuring method", applying this to all possible parts of the cycle, and measure the end-to-end outcome, the whole perspective changes. You are not "outsourcing" your skills to a tool, but "re-establishing" the progressive loops. The first observations are disappointing: your speed-up is less effect, and now it takes 6 hours to finish the task, due to the overhead of information transforming between different stages of the cycle.

Then the magic starts to work. Though "sub-optimal", it's still a 4-5x boost, and the boost is now applying to other parts of the work: doc drafting, context management, system alignment, spec check, design review, tests and secuirty checks, you name it. And the best part its, some of these can now be executed in parallel, if you get the workflow designs right - also doable with this new method.

More, in this scope, 3-hour vs 6-hour is merely a slight difference, far less than 2x. Consider the traditional flow having multiple stakeholders. The 2-week sprint is the dream. In reality you have to align their thoughts, time, do survey, generate reports and wait for feedback, especially in high-context environments. The major difference is not on the hours, but on the pace aligments. 3-days is actually 1-week consider human buffer. And you need another week for story points, reviews, meetings, debates and explaining everyone's thoughts.

Now everything fits in one, at most two days. The 6 hour implementation allows you to fit the code changes in one day, and get immediate feeback the same day. Your ask of capturing everthing during executing keeps you documents always updated, and now part of the review process and kick-off in parallel. You can easily fit your stakeholder's schedule since you can execute in fragments, showing whatever you have, and prompt your system into next stage before weekend.

A feature that needs 3 sprints = 6 weeks, is now 2 days, and you can focus on whoever's present, and work multiple tasks at the same time.

All of this wouldn't happen, if you get tunnel vision and focus on how many hours you can release from a single task. This is optimization.
Optimization is for well-scoped, proficiency oriented problems. But when facing methodological change, it is not "how 'fast' are you producing" but "'how' are you producing". Each part of the improvment is multiplied, however small, accumulates into a huge multiplier on the overall cycle.

This, the multiplied, is fundamentally different. As shitty as it may seemed, the final outcome will never be achieved pure optimization.
